## Summary

The "Inside Every" section argues that agent setups do not transfer between
people. Two colleagues compared their Codex configurations and found opposite
shapes: one built detailed planning, context organisation, and supervision of
outputs; the other set up reminders and messages so an item "can just get done"
without her tracking it. The piece's claim is that a setup mirrors how its owner
thinks and what they are actually accountable for — you can borrow someone's
ideas but not their workload, their brain, or their life. Since the tool is
open-ended enough to live in a single chat thread or on top of an elaborate
workspace, the real question is what you are setting it up for. The author's
answer was to have the agent interview her: she asked Codex to help design her
workspace by questioning her about her work, her needs, and the decisions she
wanted the system to support, and it turned the answers into a proposed desktop
architecture and set of pinned threads. She then handed the method, not the
setup, to a colleague. The actual prompt is behind the paywall.

## Takeaways for our use case

- Copying another practitioner's agent configuration wholesale usually fails,
  because it was fitted to their workload and habits rather than to the job.
- Borrow the method someone used to build their setup, not the setup itself.
- Before configuring anything, answer "set it up for what?" — which decisions do
  you want the system to support?
- A cheap way in: ask the agent to interview you about your work and needs, then
  have it propose a structure from your answers rather than guessing at one.
- Setups differ on a few concrete axes worth deciding explicitly: what context
  the agent gets, what it can handle alone, which tasks deserve a standing
  process, and when it must ask permission.
- The interview method transferred between two people even though the resulting
  setups did not.

## Candidate patterns / evidence

- → agent-interviews-you: the author told Codex she wanted help designing her
  workspace and asked it to interview her; its questions about her work and the
  decisions she wanted supported became a proposed architecture and pinned
  threads.

  found it "was built around a person, not a job", and two experienced users'
  setups diverged completely — detailed planning and supervision versus
  fire-and-forget reminders.
